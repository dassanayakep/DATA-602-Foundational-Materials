Module I: Probability¶

Learning Objectives¶

Upon completion of this module, students will be able to:

  • Formalize probability spaces using set-theoretic and axiomatic frameworks.
  • Derive and apply fundamental probability laws from Kolmogorov axioms.
  • Analyze dependence structures via conditional probability and independence.
  • Construct and interpret Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, and contingency tables.
  • Apply combinatorial methods to finite probability spaces.
  • Compute probabilities using classical, conditional, and empirical frameworks.
  • Translate verbal uncertainty problems into formal probabilistic models.

Introduction¶

Probability theory provides the mathematical foundation for reasoning under uncertainty. It underlies modern statistical inference, stochastic modeling, and predictive analytics.

Applications include:

  • Forecasting systems (weather, traffic, demand)
  • Financial risk modeling
  • Machine learning and Bayesian inference
  • Reliability engineering
  • Epidemiology and population dynamics

A central objective of probability theory is to assign a coherent measure to uncertainty that is internally consistent and mathematically well-defined.

Probability Foundations¶

Basic Concepts¶

A random experiment is a well-defined process whose outcome is uncertain.

Let:

  • $\Omega$ = sample space
  • $\omega \in \Omega$ = outcome
  • $A \subseteq \Omega$ = event

Sample Space¶

The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes.

Event¶

An event $A$ is any subset of $\Omega$, i.e. $A \in 2^\Omega$.

Classical Probability¶

If all outcomes are equally likely:

$$ P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|} $$

This is valid under a uniform probability measure on finite spaces.

Example 1.1 (Die Roll)¶

Let:

$$ \Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\} $$

Let $A = \{2,4,6\}$.

Then:

$$ P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} $$

Example 1.2 (Coin Tossing)¶

Three coin flips:

$$ |\Omega| = 2^3 = 8 $$

This follows from the multiplication principle.

Theorem 1 (Multiplication Principle)¶

If a process consists of $m$ independent stages where stage $i$ has $r_i$ outcomes, then:

$$ |\Omega| = \prod_{i=1}^{m} r_i $$

Proof¶

The sample space is the Cartesian product:

$$ \Omega = S_1 \times S_2 \times \cdots \times S_m $$

Thus:

$$ |\Omega| = \prod_{i=1}^{m} |S_i| $$

$\blacksquare$

Axioms of Probability (Kolmogorov)¶

A probability measure $P$ satisfies:

  1. Non-negativity $$ P(A) \ge 0 $$

  2. Normalization $$ P(\Omega) = 1 $$

  3. Additivity If $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$, then: $$ P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i) $$

Derived Result: Complement Rule¶

$$ P(A^c) = 1 - P(A) $$

Proof¶

Since $A \cup A^c = \Omega$,

$$ P(A) + P(A^c) = 1 $$

$\blacksquare$

Conditional Probability and Set Operations¶

Intersection¶

$$ A \cap B $$

represents simultaneous occurrence.

Union¶

$$ A \cup B $$

represents occurrence of at least one event.

Addition Rule¶

$$ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) $$

Proof Sketch¶

Decompose:

$$ A \cup B = A \cup (B \setminus A) $$

where $B\setminus A= \{x∈B:x \notin A\}$

Apply disjoint additivity.

Mutually Exclusive Events¶

$$ A \cap B = \emptyset \Rightarrow P(A \cap B) = 0 $$

Conditional Probability¶

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0 $$

Independence¶

$$ P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) $$

Equivalently:

$$ P(A \mid B) = P(A) $$

Law of Total Probability¶

If $\{B_i\}$ partitions $\Omega$:

$$ P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)P(B_i) $$

Proof¶

$$ A = \bigcup_i (A \cap B_i) $$

Apply additivity:

$$ P(A) = \sum_i P(A \cap B_i) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)P(B_i) $$

$\blacksquare$

De Morgan’s Laws¶

$$ (A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c $$

$$ (A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c $$

Counting Theory¶

Generalized Multiplication Rule¶

If $m_i$ steps have $r_i$ outcomes:

$$ \prod_{i=1}^k r_i^{m_i} $$

If you are selecting r number of objects from an n number of total objects, we could compute the total number of arrangements using either permutation or combination formulas. The key difference between permutations and combinations lies in the importance of order:

Permutations¶

Permutations are used when the order of the element’s matters. This means that changing the order creates a different arrangement. When selecting r objects from a total of n object and the selection order matters and no repetitions allowed, total number of arrangements can be calculated using:

$$ P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!} $$

Order matters

Combinations¶

Combinations, on the other hand, are used when the order of the elements does not matter. In this case, different arrangements of the same elements are considered the same. When selecting r objects from a total of n object and the selection order doesn’t matter and no repetitions allowed, total number of arrangements can be calculated using:

$$ C(n,r) = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} $$

Order does not matter

Theorem: Symmetry¶

$$ C(n,r) = C(n,n-r) $$

Proof¶

Direct algebraic equivalence:

$$ \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!r!} $$

$\blacksquare$

Permutation example¶

You have 5 letters A, B C, D E. You are selecting 3 letters

(with out repetition) to create a password.

How many distinct possible passwords can you create?

In this case, the arrangements of the letters in the password

"ABC" (like "ABC," "ACB," "BAC," etc.) are considered distinct.

Hence when counting the total number of arrangements,

we use permutation formula where n = 5 and r = 3,

$$ 5P3 = \frac{5!}{(5-3)!} $$

Combination example¶

You have 5 people named A, B C, D E. You are selecting 3 people

(with out repetition) to create committee.

How many distinct possible committees can you create?

In this case, the arrangements of the letters in the password "ABC"

(like "ABC," "ACB," "BAC," etc.) are considered same.

Hence, we do not need to count them as distinct possibilities.

Therefore, we must use combination formula to get the

total number of arrangements:

$$ 5C3 = \frac{5!}{3!(5-3)!} $$

Example (Permutation)¶

Selecting ordered top 3 from 30 athletes:

$$ P(30,3) = \frac{30!}{27!} $$

Example (Combination)¶

Selecting committee of size 3:

$$ C(30,3) = \frac{30!}{3!27!} $$

Probability via Counting¶

Probabilit of an event A: $$ P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)} $$

where counts are often computed via permutations/combinations.

Contingency Tables and Tree Diagrams¶

Empirical Probability¶

$$ P(A) = \frac{\text{frequency of } A}{\text{total observations}} $$

Conditional Structure in Tables¶

Joint probabilities:

$$ P(A \cap B) $$

Marginals:

$$ P(A) = \sum_B P(A \cap B) $$

Tree Diagram Decomposition¶

For sequential events:

$$ P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) $$

Paths multiply along branches.

Interpretation¶

  • Horizontal structure: marginalization
  • Vertical structure: conditioning
  • Full tree: joint factorization

When collecting data in real life, we could tally our results into a two-way table and use the tally results to compute probabilities. This approach is often referred as empirical approach of computing probabilities, and the two-way table is called contingency table.

Conceptual Summary¶

This module constructs a unified probability framework:

  • Finite sample spaces → combinatorial measures
  • Axioms → probability as measure theory
  • Conditional probability → ratio structure
  • Independence → factorization property
  • Counting rules → discrete measure computation

These ideas form the foundation for:

  • random variables
